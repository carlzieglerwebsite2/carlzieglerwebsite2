The survey idea

Twenty-one arcsecond pixels meet a four-meter telescope.

TESS watches nearly the whole sky with 21″ pixels, ideal for discovering transits around bright nearby stars but large enough to blend many sources together. SOAR’s High-Resolution Camera, HRCam, resolves companions tens to hundreds of times closer than a single TESS pixel can distinguish.

Why speckle imaging?

Freeze the atmosphere, then recover the diffraction limit.

Each target is recorded in hundreds of very short exposures. Atmospheric turbulence rearranges the speckles from frame to frame, but the binary-star signal persists. Combining the frames in Fourier space recovers separations, position angles, and brightness contrasts far below ordinary seeing.

What a detection looks like

A companion leaves two symmetric fingerprints.

In a speckle autocorrelation function, the target star produces the bright central peak. A companion creates a pair of mirrored peaks at the measured separation and position angle. This symmetry is a mathematical feature of the method—the companion itself appears only once on the sky.

First, set the scale

One TESS pixel contains a great deal of sky.

A TESS pixel is 21″ on a side. The 2.8″ autocorrelation field used below is 7.5 times narrower and covers only about 1.8% as much sky area. Within that small patch, SOAR reaches roughly 0.04″ resolution.

Every additional star whose light falls into the target’s TESS pixel or photometric aperture adds flux to the light curve. That extra light dilutes the fractional brightness drop: the observed transit becomes shallower, so—without a correction—the planet appears smaller than it really is.

The Revision 39 population result

The deficit is deepest nearby—and begins recovering near 100 AU.

The controlled analysis compares 162 candidate visual companions around 1,199 dwarf primaries with an empirical q ≥ 0.4 field population passed through each target’s measured sensitivity. Fixed projected-separation counts establish the deficit; a forward model then locates the recovery in physical semimajor axis.

A simple shift toward 100 AU is strongly disfavored.

The fully specified, multiplicity-conserving 100 AU shift performs far worse than smooth suppression and recovery. In the Gaia range, 88 companions are observed versus 72.9 expected—a broadly compatible wide population once field normalization and Gaia/TOI selection uncertainties are included.

07 · Conditional planet yield
17.8%

estimated yield lost in the directly measured q ≥ 0.4 domain

Conditionally interpreting the recovery law as relative planet occurrence gives a 17.8% lost yield for a volume-limited population and 25.5% for a magnitude-limited population. Extrapolating below the measured mass-ratio limit gives 24.3% and 31.3%. These are population translations—not end-to-end TESS injection–recovery measurements.

08 · Blended-light corrections
×1.070

median primary-host planet-radius correction

For 630 systems with at least one source inside 3″, the median radius correction is ×1.070, the 90th percentile is ×1.326, and the maximum is ×1.456. At fixed planet mass, the median and 90th-percentile corrections reduce inferred density by 18% and 57%.

09 · Hidden hierarchies
7

wide systems show radial-velocity evidence for unseen inner structure.

Among jointly covered hosts with a SOAR component at 300–5,000 AU, nine trigger the paired Gaia RVS-noise threshold and seven of those are not RUWE flagged. Because the imaged wide star cannot cause short-timescale velocity noise, these are candidates for an additional inner subsystem.

10 · M-dwarf check

The qualitative deficit extends to small stars.

Among 89 M-dwarf hosts, SOAR sees 2 sources inside 50 AU where 16.7 are expected, for a relative frequency of 0.160. The sample supports the same qualitative pattern, but is not yet a calibrated common suppression amplitude across stellar masses.

The survey series

From the first 542 hosts to a 2,982-target census.

Papers I and II established the survey and its first population result. Revision 39 of the third paper consolidates every HRCam epoch, calibrates the field comparison for resolved-target selection and wide-binary mass ratios, fits the recovery in physical semimajor axis, and uses Gaia to test whether the missing close systems were merely redistributed outward.

I2020

542 TESS hosts · 117 companion systems

SOAR TESS Survey. I: Sculpting of TESS planetary systems by stellar companions

The opening paper measured companions, supplied planet-radius corrections for blended systems, and found an initial deficit of close binaries together with an apparent wide-companion enhancement that motivated deeper scrutiny.

Carl Ziegler, Andrei Tokovinin, César Briceño, James Mang, Nicholas M. Law, Andrew W. Mann.

II2021

589 new TESS hosts · combined population analysis

SOAR TESS Survey. II: The impact of stellar companions on planetary populations

The second paper added 589 targets and 89 companion systems, then used the combined survey to measure nearly factor-of-seven suppression in close solar-type binaries, test the result for M dwarfs, and trace the wide-binary excess to false-positive contamination.

Carl Ziegler, Andrei Tokovinin, Madelyn Latiolais, César Briceño, Nicholas M. Law, Andrew W. Mann.

III2026

2,982 unique TIC targets · 431 post-Paper-II measurements

SOAR TESS Survey. III: The Cumulative Companion Census and Close-Binary Demographics of TESS Objects of Interest

The cumulative draft reports 729 latest-epoch source detections around 688 hosts. Revision 39 measures S₅₀ = 0.292 and S₁₀₀ = 0.362, locates the HRCam-only recovery midpoint at 106 AU, finds the wide Gaia population broadly field-compatible, and strongly rejects a multiplicity-conserving shift of the field distribution toward 100 AU.

Carl Ziegler, Andrei Tokovinin, César Briceño, Nicholas M. Law, Andrew W. Mann.

A community follow-up resource

Measurements built to travel with each planet candidate.

Resolved companions, contrast limits, and radius-correction factors support the wider TESS Follow-up Observing Program. The measurements help observers prioritize targets, identify blended eclipsing binaries, and interpret confirmed planets with the correct stellar light.
