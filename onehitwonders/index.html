The observing problem

A planet can transit once, then disappear from the schedule.

TESS's original observing strategy moved to a new sector every 27.4 days. Across most of the sky, that strongly favored multiple transits for planets with periods shorter than about 14 days. Longer-period planets could appear only once, leaving the orbital period poorly constrained even when the transit itself was convincing.

A thousand one-time events

The survey simulation predicted 904 ± 65 detectable single-transit planets, comparable to independent pre-launch estimates and concentrated at longer periods than the multi-transit TESS population.

The single-transit problem

A long-period planet can cross its star once in the available data. Without another transit, its ephemeris rapidly becomes uncertain.

A valuable corner of parameter space

Longer-period planets around bright stars fill a sparse part of the transiting-planet census and are unusually valuable for mass measurements and atmospheric characterization.

The recovery engine

Watch many windows. Trigger on the one that dips.

The draft survey design combines a dedicated 0.5-m telescope, period estimates from the TESS transit and stellar properties, automated scheduling, and real-time photometry. Instead of staring at one uncertain target all night, the telescope cycles through several plausible transit windows until the data themselves tell it to stop.

Deep Sky West · Rowe, New Mexico2,200 m elevation · roughly 250 photometric nights per year in the survey design

Survey hardware

Purpose-built for long photometric windows.

  • PlaneWave CDK-200.5-m corrected Dall-Kirkham optical tube
  • ~0.8 deg² fieldf/4.5 reduced beam for efficient differential photometry
  • Apogee U16M16-megapixel camera at 0.82 arcsec per pixel
  • Engineered diffuserspreads bright-star light into a stable top-hat PSF for precision photometry
  • Python automationscripted scheduling, calibration, photometry, and trigger logic
01

Estimate

Fit the single TESS transit and stellar density to turn transit duration and geometry into a probability distribution for orbital period.

02

Schedule

Build a nightly list of plausible transit windows. The design expected 6–8 targets on a typical night, with 12 as a hard limit.

03

Cycle

Observe the targets at long cadence while on-the-fly aperture photometry compares each star with stable nearby references.

04

Trigger

Consecutive significant low points trigger continuous short-cadence observations, securing the transit and collapsing the ephemeris uncertainty.

Original survey forecast

What could a single dedicated site recover?

The draft paper used Monte Carlo populations and a simulated New Mexico observing season to estimate the opportunity. These numbers describe the survey forecast, not a claim about the final realized yield.

01 · Recover

Turn a one-off event into an orbit.

One Hit Wonders uses targeted ground-based photometry to search predicted transit windows. A recovered event sharply improves the orbital period and makes future follow-up schedulable.

The dedicated telescope at Deep Sky West lets the survey pursue long windows and repeated opportunities that are awkward for heavily subscribed facilities.

02 · Characterize

An atmospheric bridge to cooler worlds.

Most early transmission work concentrated on intensely irradiated hot Jupiters. Moving to longer periods opens planets at lower equilibrium temperatures, where different molecules and condensates become important. Around bright stars, recovered ephemerides can enable mass measurements and atmospheric observations that connect hot exoplanets with the cooler giant planets of our own Solar System.

The team

Built around hands-on follow-up.

The survey was developed during Carl Ziegler's Dunlap Fellowship and began observing in September 2019, combining small-telescope operations, automated photometry, and student-accessible exoplanet follow-up.
