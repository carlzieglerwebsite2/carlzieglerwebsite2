About

Finding signal in crowded skies.

Space telescopes discover planets by measuring tiny changes in starlight, but neighboring stars can blur the picture. The cumulative SOAR TESS Survey finds 3.5 times fewer nearby sources inside 50 AU and 2.8 times fewer inside 100 AU than a sensitivity-convolved field-binary model predicts, with the companion frequency recovering toward the field population at wider separations. The primary measurement is resolved-source frequency among selected TESS Objects of Interest; its planet-occurrence interpretation remains conditional on association, selection, and transit completeness.

Research that connects telescopes, data, and people.

I grew up in Hannibal, Missouri, earned degrees in physics and mathematics at William Jewell College and Southern Illinois University, and completed my Ph.D. in Physics and Astronomy at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. I later served as a Dunlap Postdoctoral Fellow at the University of Toronto before joining SFA in 2020.

At SFA, I lead the observatory, teach courses from introductory astronomy through astrophysics, mentor student researchers, develop public outreach programs, and build tools that let learners investigate physical systems directly.

Research

How do stars reshape planetary systems?

My research program spans survey astronomy, exoplanet validation, stellar multiplicity, robotic observing, and instrument development. The common thread is precise measurement: resolving ambiguity so that planetary populations can be interpreted correctly.

Flagship survey

SOAR TESS Survey

Speckle imaging with the 4.1-meter SOAR telescope resolves stellar neighbors hidden in TESS light curves, corrects planet radii for blended light, and reveals how close binaries reshape the detected planet population.

Survey astronomy

Robo-AO Kepler Survey

Collaborators and I obtained high-resolution images of all 3,857 Kepler planet-candidate systems. The resulting data helped identify blended stars and false positives, revise hundreds of planet radii, and support the validation of more than 1,500 Kepler and K2 planets.

Instrumentation

Robo-SOAR

I designed and built the testbed for a low-cost natural-guide-star adaptive-optics system for the SOAR telescope. The project pairs a novel reflective wavefront-sensor design with robotic control software to expand access to high-resolution imaging.

Long-period planets

Recovering the “one-hit wonders” of TESS

Many long-period planets appear only once in a short TESS observing window. Ground-based follow-up can recover later transits, refine their orbital periods, and preserve them for future atmospheric characterization. My work combines robotic observing, precision photometry, and coordinated follow-up to recover these temperate systems.

Featured project

Gravitas turns gravitational physics into an experiment.

Gravitas is an open-source, browser-based astronomy sandbox that I designed for students and the public. Learners can build orbital systems, change physical parameters, inspect energy conservation, simulate compact-object mergers, generate transit light curves, visualize spacetime, and translate orbital motion and gravitational-wave signatures into sound.

The project is used in introductory astronomy instruction and supports inquiry-driven activities in which students predict, test, revise, and explain rather than simply watch an animation.

  • Interactive orbital dynamics
  • Black holes and compact objects
  • Energy and collision analysis
  • Transit photometry
  • Spacetime visualization
  • Scientific sonification

AI and scientific discovery

Using intelligent tools to make difficult science more tractable.

My interest in artificial intelligence is grounded in research practice. Modern astronomy depends on finding weak signals in enormous, noisy data sets, separating astrophysical effects from instrumental and stellar variability, and validating conclusions against independent observations. Several studies I have coauthored show how machine learning and probabilistic modeling can strengthen that process, while my own survey work emphasizes the careful measurements and bias corrections needed to keep computational results scientifically trustworthy.

Selected research

Machine learning and probabilistic methods in published exoplanet studies.

These collaborations illustrate practical uses of AI-adjacent methods in astronomy: identifying transient stellar activity, modeling correlated noise, separating activity from planetary signals, and building the validated observational context needed for reliable inference.

Convolutional neural network

Identifying stellar flares in TESS observations

In the THYME X study, our collaboration used stella, a convolutional neural network trained on TESS short-cadence data, to identify flare-contaminated measurements before analyzing the young two-planet TOI-1224 system. The work demonstrates how learned classifiers can improve the reliability of difficult time-series measurements.

Read THYME X
Gaussian process

Recovering transits around a highly variable young star

THYME II modeled TESS photometry with a periodic Gaussian process alongside the planetary transit model. Representing the star's correlated variability made it possible to characterize HIP 67522 b, then the youngest known transiting hot Jupiter.

Read THYME II
Multiwavelength probabilistic modeling

Separating stellar activity from planetary motion

The TOI-560 study applied Gaussian-process modeling to TESS light curves and used that information in a wavelength-dependent radial-velocity analysis. The combined approach helped distinguish stellar activity from the signals of two planets orbiting a young K dwarf.

Read the TOI-560 study
Ground truth and bias correction

Building the evidence trustworthy models require

The SOAR TESS Survey provides a complementary foundation for data-driven science: independently validated observations, false-positive screening, target-specific sensitivity, and explicit selection-effect corrections. Its cumulative analysis finds 24 nearby sources inside 50 AU where 84.6 are expected and uses SOAR plus Gaia to distinguish genuine close-source depletion from a simple outward redistribution of binaries.

Explore the cumulative SOAR TESS results

The common thread: computational methods are most useful when they are paired with domain knowledge, interpretable uncertainty, independent observations, and careful validation. That combination is central to how I think about the future of AI-enabled science.
